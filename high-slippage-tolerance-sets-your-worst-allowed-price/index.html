High slippage tolerance sets your worst allowed price

Does high slippage tolerance mean you get the worst price? No: it sets your swap’s minimum output; the pool sets execution, which you can verify on-chain.

The Blockheight Editors·Nov 27, 2026 · 19:17 UTC·3 min read

High slippage tolerance does not mean your swap automatically gets the worst price; it sets the minimum output your transaction will accept, while the pool determines the executed price.

  • Tolerance limits the loss the transaction can accept.
  • Actual slippage is the difference between quoted output and received output.
  • On Ethereum, a wide tolerance can increase sandwich risk in the public mempool.

Slippage tolerance sets a floor for your output

The setting can look like a price instruction because raising it lets a swap proceed through a larger price move. In practice, the contract checks whether the output meets a minimum; it does not aim for that minimum as a target.

Say a quote shows 1 token out and you set 5% tolerance. The transaction can accept output down to about 0.95 tokens; below that, it reverts. If the pool still offers close to 1 token when your swap executes, you receive close to the quote even with the wider setting.

That distinction matters in a deep, calm pool: a high tolerance alone does not worsen the pool price. The price can still change between quote and execution, and the amount you actually receive reflects that change.

Actual slippage appears in the executed amounts

To see what your swap used, find the transaction in an Ethereum block explorer and inspect the token amounts transferred. Compare the amount received with the quote you saw when submitting the swap; the quoted output minus the received output is the actual slippage in token terms.

To express that difference as a percentage, divide it by the quoted output. The explorer records what the transaction executed, but you may need your wallet or swap interface history to recover the original quote.

A wide tolerance does matter when someone can move the price before your transaction executes. On Ethereum’s public mempool, a sandwich attacker can trade before and after your swap, pushing its execution toward the minimum output you allowed. The broader the gap between the quote and that floor, the more room the transaction gives this attack to reduce your received amount.

Read the executed token amounts against your saved quote, then compare the loss with the transaction’s minimum output. That shows whether the swap merely moved with the pool or used nearly all the tolerance you allowed.
