Bridge upgrades shift what crypto users must trust

Bridge upgrades can change the contracts, signers or proofs securing transfers; check the new trust assumptions and migration steps before moving funds.

The Blockheight Editors·Sep 29, 2026·3 min read

A bridge upgrade can change who or what a user must trust to move assets between blockchains. The change may affect the contracts holding funds, the validators approving transfers or the proofs used to verify messages, so users should review the new design before sending.

A bridge connects separate chains by recording a deposit on one and authorizing a corresponding action on the other. In a lock-and-mint design, a contract holds the original asset while another chain issues a representation; in a burn-and-release flow, that representation is destroyed before the original is released. The bridge’s rules determine how those steps are verified.

For a specific example of delayed messages, read how Mantle Bridge pending transfers work. A delay can reflect the bridge’s verification or challenge process, rather than a simple wallet balance update.

What can a bridge upgrade change?

An upgrade can replace the contracts that hold or release assets, change the set of signers that approve messages, or alter how one chain proves events from another. It can also change relayer software, message formats, withdrawal delays or the process used to recover from an error.

Those changes move trust between components. A bridge that previously relied on a small signer group might add cryptographic proofs; a bridge that adopts a new proof system may rely more on its verifier contracts and the assumptions behind that system. The label “upgrade” alone does not tell users whether the change reduces risk.

Check the upgrade notice for the specific changes:

  • Which contracts or verification rules are being replaced?
  • Who can authorize the upgrade, and can that authority pause or redirect transfers?
  • Do users need to migrate tokens, update an address or wait for a new verification period?
  • How will pending deposits and withdrawals be handled?

How does an upgrade change what users trust?

Users trust the whole path that turns an action on one chain into an action on another. Depending on the design, that path may include a source-chain contract, message relayers, validators, a destination-chain verifier and an upgrade authority with permission to change the code.

A contract audit can review code, but it does not by itself establish that the signer group is independent, that the upgrade authority is constrained or that a new verifier behaves as intended. Read the published technical proposal and look for the implementation address, the approval process and the stated treatment of funds already in transit.

For readers, the practical comparison is between the old and new trust assumptions, not just between feature lists. A faster transfer may require a shorter verification window; a more decentralized verification process may take longer or require more complex proofs. The trade-off depends on which component gains authority and how users can verify its actions.

What should users check before bridging?

Use the bridge’s current official interface and confirm that its displayed network, asset and destination match the intended transfer. If an upgrade notice calls for a migration, follow the stated steps and verify contract addresses through the project’s official channels before approving a transaction.

Then check whether the bridge is accepting new transfers, how it handles pending messages and where it publishes status updates. If those details are missing, waiting for clarification is safer than assuming the old process still applies.

The next step is to compare the announced implementation and migration instructions with the bridge’s current status. Whether funds already in transit follow the old or new rules may remain unconfirmed until the operator publishes its handling plan.
