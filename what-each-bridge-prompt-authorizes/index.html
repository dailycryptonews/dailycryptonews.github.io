What Each Bridge Prompt Authorizes

What each wallet signature means when bridging tokens: connect, network, approval, transaction and claim prompts—and the spending power each one grants.

The Blockheight Editors·Nov 24, 2026 · 02:25 UTC·3 min read

A bridge transfer can show up to five wallet prompts, each with a separate purpose: connect, switch or add a network, approve or sign a permit, submit the bridge transaction, and sometimes claim on Monad. The Monad bridge is a concrete example because it transfers supported assets between Ethereum and Monad. Reading each prompt matters because an approval can grant spending power even when signing it costs no gas.

Wallet prompts grant access, spending power or a transfer

When you start a transfer, the bridge app asks your wallet for access, then requests any network change and token authorization it needs. The bridge transaction sends the transfer on the source chain; the app’s process then releases the asset on Monad, sometimes after a separate claim.

  • Connect: Shares your wallet address and balances with the app so it can prepare the transfer. It grants no token spending rights.
  • Add or switch network: Adds a chain to your wallet or changes the active chain. Confirm that the chain ID shown matches the chain you intend to use.
  • Token approval or permit: Authorizes a named spender to use a token amount. An ERC-20 approval sets an allowance for a spender contract; choose the transfer amount as the cap rather than unlimited.
  • Bridge transaction: Submits the transfer on the source chain. Depending on the bridge’s design, the tokens are locked or burned there before the corresponding assets are released on Monad.
  • Claim: Appears in some flows to complete receipt on Monad. Check the destination network, asset and amount before confirming.

An EIP-2612 permit uses an EIP-712 signature to grant an ERC-20 allowance off-chain, instead of sending an approval transaction first. Permit and Permit2 signatures cost no gas to sign, but they can still grant spending power like an on-chain approval. Read the spender, token and cap in the signature details; a zero gas cost does not make a broad allowance harmless.

A MON transfer shows where each prompt fits

Say you are moving MON from Ethereum to Monad. You connect your wallet, switch to Ethereum if needed, and approve the named spender for the amount you plan to bridge—or sign a permit if the app offers one. The bridge transaction then submits the transfer on Ethereum; after processing, the app may ask you to claim the MON on Monad.

That sequence is useful whenever you bridge tokens: connection gives the app visibility, network selection sets where the wallet acts, and approval or permit grants token access. The bridge transaction starts the cross-chain transfer; a claim prompt may finish destination-side receipt. Review each prompt’s chain, spender, asset and amount before signing.

  • Connect shares wallet information, not spending rights.
  • Network prompts change the active chain; verify the chain ID.
  • Approval and permit prompts grant spending power; keep the cap to the transfer amount.
  • Bridge and claim prompts carry out or complete the transfer.
